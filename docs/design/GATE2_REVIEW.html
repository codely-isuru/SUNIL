Section 1 · Decision required

The dim-cyan label colour, rendered three ways

The prototype has exactly two text colours: bright cyan for values, and a 55%-transparent cyan for everything else — labels, units, timestamps, status words. That second colour is used by almost every small piece of text in the interface, and it fails the accessibility contrast standard the project committed to (4.5:1 for normal text). Measured, it reaches 3.43:1 at worst.

The awkward part: on a near-black background, "dimmer" and "harder to read" are the same physical quantity. There is no version of dim cyan that is both dimmer and compliant. So the question is not whether it changes, but which replacement still feels like SUNIL. Below, the same three pieces of real interface — a stat tile, a status list and a timestamp — drawn in each candidate, on each of the three backgrounds they actually appear on in the product.

Section 1b · Also worth your eye

Body text: cyan → ice

Separate from the decision above, and the change you are most likely to notice. The prototype sets all text to full cyan. That passes contrast comfortably — this is not a compliance fix — but the prototype only ever displays about thirty words. The real product shows audit tables, log lines, settings forms and error messages. Saturated cyan at paragraph length on near-black is tiring to read, and it spends the accent colour on everything, so nothing can stand out.

Section 1c

The one real trade-off, and what I would pick

Both options pass. The difference is not how they look — at 1× they are very close — it is what each one permits later.

Option B keeps the translucent idiom, which is genuinely part of the prototype's character: the colour is cyan seen through the interface rather than painted on it. It also happens to score marginally better over the glow, because transparent text brightens along with whatever is behind it.

The cost is that it leaves transparency legal as a text colour. A transparent colour has no fixed contrast ratio — it only has one once you know exactly what is painted behind it. Today there are three backgrounds and all three were measured. This phase introduces several more: a raised menu surface, a modal, a hover tint, a selected-row highlight. The moment a label lands on one of those, its ratio changes, and nothing in the build catches it — there is no test that can check a colour whose value depends on what is underneath. It would simply become non-compliant quietly, and the next time anyone found out would be an accessibility audit.

Option A is a fixed colour. Measure it once and it stays measured, on every surface that will ever exist. That is the entire difference.

My recommendation

Option A — the opaque #1BA3BC — on the grounds of durability rather than appearance. It is the one that cannot silently regress, and label colour is not somewhere you want a quiet failure across twenty-two screens.

That said, this is a judgement about your product's character as much as its compliance, and the decision is yours, not mine. If the translucent quality matters to you more than the guarantee, Option B is a legitimate choice and is fully specified — I would then add a rule that transparency is permitted for this one token and nothing else, and ask QA to re-measure it whenever a new surface colour is introduced. That is a process control rather than a build control, which is weaker, but it is honest and it works if it is followed.

What I need from you

  1. D-1: Option A, Option B, or "leave it failing and record the exception" — the third is available and would be documented as a deliberate accessibility deviation, not hidden.
  2. D-4 (body text cyan → ice): confirm or reject. Independent of D-1.

Nothing else in Stage 3 is blocked on you. Section 2 is for information — flag anything that looks wrong and it goes back to the specialist who owns it.

Section 2 · For information

What Stage 3 produced

a · The three decisions the Architect was asked to make

These were routed up from the requirements as questions the analyst deliberately refused to answer alone. All three are now settled and written down as decision records.

ADR-001 · Permissions

Plain strings, no wildcards

Permissions are simple resource:action rows in the database — user:invite, secret:rotate — and the check is a straight "is it in the list?". No pattern matching, because a pattern-matching bug is a privilege-escalation bug.

Only one owner account can exist, and that is enforced three deep: the setup script, the service layer, and a database index that rejects a second one even if the code is bypassed.
ADR-002 · Job durability

Redis writes to disk every second

The job queue is configured to append every change to disk with a one-second flush, on a named volume, and is forbidden from evicting data under memory pressure — eviction would silently delete queued jobs.

Worst case if the machine is pulled apart mid-run: about one second of work. A normal stop and start loses nothing at all, and job history lives in the database, so it survives even a total queue wipe.
ADR-003 · Sign-in

The pattern, not the library

The architecture named "Lucia-style" sessions. Lucia was discontinued by its author and turned into documentation, so depending on it would tie your sign-in to an abandoned package.

The documented pattern is implemented directly; every cryptographic part comes from a maintained library — argon2id password hashing via @node-rs, chosen partly because it installs on Windows without a build toolchain. No home-made cryptography anywhere.

The target architecture was written for the finished system. Phase 1 departs from it in exactly five places, each argued rather than assumed.

The raw count looks alarming and mostly is not. Twenty of those thirty-two are decorative canvas ornament — the scanline veil, the rotating HUD arcs, the orbital ring — which carry no information and are exempt from the standard. The remaining twelve are the same three colours each measured against four different backgrounds, so stripped back to things whose role actually imposes a threshold there are three real defects:

  • The dim-cyan label colour at 3.43:1 — Section 1 of this page.
  • The "offline" status dot at 1.85:1, which is the sole visual carrier of "this connector is down". Corrected one shade lighter; visually almost indistinguishable.
  • The single border colour at 1.37:1 — fine as a decorative panel edge, unusable as the outline of a text field or a button. Split into two tokens so the decorative one cannot leak onto a control.

Every colour locked in the architecture document passes unchanged — the cyan #22d3ee, the background #030712, the panel fill, the amber and the green. Nothing in your brand identity had to move.

d · Two defects inherited from the prototype

Both were found by reading the prototype closely during design. Neither is anyone's mistake — they are the kind of thing a demo gets away with and a product does not.

Defect 1 · The clock

Pinned to Melbourne

The prototype's clock is hard-coded to Australia/Melbourne and prints the fixed label "MELBOURNE · AEST". You operate on Hobart.

The two share an offset most of the year, which is exactly why this would survive casual testing and then be wrong during the daylight-saving changeover and in every exported timestamp. Fixed properly: the zone is a setting, defaults to Australia/Hobart, the abbreviation is derived rather than typed, and no part of the product is allowed to format a time without saying which zone it means.
Defect 2 · The sphere

Runs 2.4× fast on a good screen

The animation advances its clock by a fixed amount per frame drawn, not per second elapsed. On the 60 Hz screen it was written for, that is correct.

On a 144 Hz laptop the whole thing runs 2.4× too fast; on a busy machine dropping frames, half speed. The rebuilt component measures real elapsed time instead, and clamps it so that returning to a background tab does not make the sphere lurch.

e · What your dashboard will actually look like

Setting this expectation now rather than at the demo

The prototype dashboard is full — revenue, subscribers, ad spend, a content queue, a task list, six connector lamps. Every one of those numbers is typed into the file by hand. None of it is connected to anything, and Phase 1 builds none of the features that would connect it.

So the Phase 1 dashboard will be mostly empty, on purpose. It has a permanent banner saying which phase you are in and what does not exist yet; the presence sphere; one panel of genuinely live data — database, queue, worker, scheduler status, really queried; a plain list of what is coming and in which phase; and three links to the things that do work.

There are no placeholder metrics and no sample charts, because the project rule is that nothing mocked may be presented as complete. The alternative — copying the prototype's layout and filling it with invented numbers — would give you a screen that lies at a glance, and the first time you trusted one of those numbers it would cost more than the empty space does. The empty space is the honest state of the system, and it will fill in over Phases 2 and 3.
